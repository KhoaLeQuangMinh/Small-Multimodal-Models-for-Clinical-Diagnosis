# Tuberculosis - Canonical Clinical Profile

## 1. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL EXPOSURES & TRANSMISSION
- **Etiologic Agent & Microbiology:** Caused by the *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex (principally *M. tuberculosis*, and rarely *M. bovis*, *M. africanum*). An obligate aerobe, slow-growing (generation time 15–20 hours), acid-fast bacillus (AFB) characterized by a waxy, lipid-dense cell wall rich in mycolic acids that resists standard Gram staining and acid-alcohol decolorization.
- **Transmission Mechanism:** Exclusively airborne via inhalation of microscopic droplet nuclei (1–5 $\mu$m in diameter) generated when a person with infectious pulmonary or laryngeal tuberculosis coughs, sneezes, speaks, or sings. Droplets remain suspended in ambient air for hours. Transmission requires prolonged, close indoor contact in poorly ventilated spaces (crowded households, prisons, homeless shelters, refugee camps).
- **Global Endemicity & Reservoirs:** Humans are the sole maintenance reservoir. Endemic worldwide with the highest disease burden in South-East Asia (45% of global cases), sub-Saharan Africa (23%), and the Western Pacific (e.g., India, Indonesia, China, Philippines, Pakistan, Nigeria, South Africa).
- **Major Host Susceptibility Factors:**
  - **HIV/AIDS:** The single most potent risk factor for progression from latent infection to active disease; TB remains the leading cause of death among people living with HIV worldwide.
  - Undernutrition, Type 2 diabetes mellitus (triples reactivation risk), silicosis (30-fold increased risk), chronic kidney disease, tobacco smoking, heavy alcohol use, and therapeutic immunosuppression (anti-TNF-$\alpha$ biologics, systemic corticosteroids, chemotherapy).
- **Latency & Incubation:**
  - Approximately 23% of the world's population (~1.7 billion people) harbors Latent Tuberculosis Infection (LTBI).
  - Primary disease may develop within 1–2 years in 5% of exposed immunocompetent hosts, while another 5% experience reactivation decades later when cellular immunity wanes.

## 2. CARDINAL CLINICAL SIGNS & TIMELINE
The clinical hallmark is an indolent, insidious progression evolving over weeks to months:
- **1. Cardinal Systemic "B-Symptoms":**
  - **Chronic Cough:** Persistent cough lasting $> 2–3$ weeks, initially non-productive and dry, progressing to mucoid or purulent sputum production.
  - **Hemoptysis:** Ranges from blood-streaked sputum to life-threatening massive hemoptysis resulting from cavitary erosion of bronchial vessels or rupture of an aneurysm of the pulmonary artery branch within a cavity (**Rasmussen aneurysm**).
  - **Fever & Diaphoresis:** Low-grade, remittent fevers characteristically spiking in the late afternoon or evening, accompanied by drenching, soaking nocturnal sweats requiring change of bedclothes.
  - **Progressive "Consumption":** Marked unintentional weight loss, cachexia, profound anorexia, and chronic fatigue.
  - Pleuritic chest pain (from subpleural parenchymal involvement or tuberculous pleurisy) and progressive dyspnea.
- **2. Extrapulmonary Tuberculosis (EPTB - 20–40% of Cases):**
  - **Tuberculous Lymphadenitis (Scrofula):** Most frequent form of EPTB; presents as painless, firm, matted, non-erythematous cervical or supraclavicular lymph node enlargement that may break down into cold abscesses with chronically draining fistulae.
  - **Pleural Tuberculosis:** Acute or subacute pleuritic pain and unilateral dullness to percussion secondary to an exudative effusion with high protein and lymphocytic predominance.
  - **Skeletal TB (Pott Disease / Tuberculous Spondylitis):** Chronic localized thoracolumbar back pain, vertebral body collapse causing sharp angular kyphosis (**gibbus deformity**), and psoas "cold abscess" tracking into the groin.
  - **Central Nervous System TB (Tuberculous Meningitis):** Subacute onset of persistent headache, low-grade fever, vomiting, personality changes, progressing to cranial nerve palsies (CN III, VI, VII due to thick basilar gelatinous exudate), confusion, and coma.
  - **Miliary Tuberculosis:** Hematogenous dissemination presenting with high fever, severe wasting, hepatosplenomegaly, and choroidal tubercles on fundoscopic examination.
  - **Abdominal TB:** Chronic abdominal pain, palpable right iliac fossa mass (ileocecal hypertrophic TB), alternating diarrhea and constipation, or tuberculous peritonitis with ascites and a "doughy" abdomen.

## 3. ADMISSION LAB & BEDSIDE IMAGING HALLMARKS
- **Complete Blood Count (CBC):**
  - **Anemia of Chronic Disease:** Mild-to-moderate normocytic normochromic anemia ($Hb\ 8–11\text{ g/dL}$) with low serum iron and elevated ferritin.
  - **Leukocyte Profile:** Total WBC is typically **normal or only mildly elevated** ($6,000–12,000/\mu\text{L}$). Marked leukocytosis ($> 15,000/\mu\text{L}$) is distinctly unusual and indicates secondary bacterial infection. A characteristically elevated monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio and persistent lymphopenia.
  - **Reactive Thrombocytosis:** Platelets frequently elevated ($400,000–600,000/\mu\text{L}$), reflecting chronic granulomatous inflammation.
  - Absence of peripheral eosinophilia.
- **Inflammatory & Metabolic Panels:**
  - **Markedly Elevated ESR:** Erythrocyte sedimentation rate is characteristically high ($> 60–100\text{ mm/h}$); C-reactive protein (CRP) is elevated.
  - Hypercalcemia occurs in up to 10–25% of active cases due to autonomous 1-$\alpha$-hydroxylase production by epithelioid histiocytes in granulomas.
  - Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) causing hyponatremia is common in pulmonary and CNS TB.
- **Admission Sputum & Rapid Molecular Diagnostics:**
  - **Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) Smear Microscopy:** Sputum Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) or auramine-O fluorescent stain detects bright red or glowing rod-shaped bacilli (requires $\ge 5,000–10,000$ bacilli/mL for sensitivity).
  - **Xpert MTB/RIF (Cartridge-Based NAAT):** WHO-recommended initial diagnostic test at admission; detects *M. tuberculosis* complex DNA and mutations conferring rifampicin resistance (*rpoB* gene) within 2 hours with $>90\%$ sensitivity.
- **Bedside Imaging (Chest Radiography & Point-of-Care Ultrasound):**
  - **Chest Radiography (Post-Primary / Reactivation TB):** Heterogeneous patchy consolidation, nodular densities, and thick-walled **cavitary lesions localized predominantly to the apical and posterior segments of the upper lobes** and superior segments of the lower lobes, often surrounded by "tree-in-bud" satellite micronodules.
  - **Primary TB:** Mid-to-lower zone infiltrates with unilateral hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Ghon complex).
  - **Miliary TB:** Distinctive, uniform, diffuse bilateral 1–2 mm micronodular interstitial opacities resembling millet seeds evenly distributed from lung apex to base.
  - **POCUS (FOCUSED Assessment with Sonography for HIV-associated TB - FASH):** Detection of pericardial effusion, pleural effusion, ascites, splenic microabscesses, and enlarged abdominal lymph nodes ($> 1.5\text{ cm}$) at bedside.

## 4. DIFFERENTIAL RULE-OUT CRITERIA (Look-Alikes)
- **Melioidosis (*Burkholderia pseudomallei*):**
  - *Differentiator:* Can present with upper lobe cavitary pneumonia and hemoptysis in tropical Asia and Australia.
  - *Exclusion Rule:* Acute pulmonary melioidosis presents with an acute toxic fever evolving over days, marked neutrophilic leukocytosis (WBC $> 15,000–30,000/\mu\text{L}$), high procalcitonin, and visceral/splenic "honeycomb" microabscesses, primarily in diabetics. TB presents with an insidious course ($> 3$ weeks), normal/mild WBC, and positive AFB/GeneXpert.
- **Paragonimiasis (Lung Fluke):**
  - *Differentiator:* Chronic cough, rusty-brown hemoptysis, and cavitary or ring-shaped cystic lesions on chest radiography.
  - *Exclusion Rule:* Paragonimiasis is preceded by raw freshwater crab/crayfish ingestion and features **marked peripheral blood eosinophilia ($> 15–30\%$)**, which is completely absent in pulmonary tuberculosis.
- **Bronchogenic Carcinoma:**
  - *Differentiator:* Cavitary lung mass, chronic cough, hemoptysis, cachexia in older smokers.
  - *Exclusion Rule:* Carcinoma features thick irregular cavitary walls with eccentrically located lumina, absence of fever/night sweats, negative tuberculin/IGRA, and malignant cytology on bronchoscopy.
- **Fungal Pneumonias (Histoplasmosis, Paracoccidioidomycosis, Coccidioidomycosis):**
  - *Differentiator:* Chronic cavitary pulmonary lesions with systemic wasting.
  - *Exclusion Rule:* Paracoccidioidomycosis features mulberry-like ulcerative mucosal lesions in the oropharynx and pilot's wheel budding yeast cells.
- **Disease-Specific Exclusion Criteria:**
  - Acute onset of severe fever and respiratory symptoms evolving in $< 48–72$ hours rules OUT uncomplicated tuberculosis.
  - Marked peripheral eosinophilia ($> 1,500/\mu\text{L}$ or $> 15\%$) strongly argues against active tuberculosis and points to parasitic lung disease or fungal infection.
  - Rapid resolution of pulmonary consolidation within 5–7 days of standard beta-lactam therapy rules OUT tuberculosis.

## 5. EMPIRICAL DRUG RESPONSE CLUES
- **First-Line Standard Antitubercular Therapy (RIPE Protocol):**
  - WHO-standardized short-course regimen consists of an **Intensive Phase** (2 months of Rifampicin [R], Isoniazid [H], Pyrazinamide [Z], and Ethambutol [E]) followed by a **Continuation Phase** (4 months of Rifampicin and Isoniazid).
  - **Clinical Response Timeline:** Defervescence, reduction of nocturnal sweating, and improved appetite characteristically occur within **2 to 4 weeks** of initiating RIPE therapy. Sputum smear conversion to negative typically occurs by the end of month 2.
- **Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) Supplementation:** Mandatory co-administration of pyridoxine (25–50 mg daily) to prevent isoniazid-induced peripheral neuropathy.
- **Paradoxical Reactions / Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS):**
  - In HIV-coinfected patients, initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) within 2 weeks of starting TB treatment may trigger exuberant inflammatory worsening (fever, enlarging lymph nodes, expanding infiltrates) requiring corticosteroid therapy.
- **Antibacterial Masking Hazard:** Fluoroquinolones (moxifloxacin, levofloxacin) possess potent anti-mycobacterial activity; transient improvement after empirical quinolones for "community-acquired pneumonia" frequently leads to dangerous diagnostic delay.
